# The interview

Read this when you start step 2 of creating a skill.

The skill is only as good as the judgment you get out of the person. They know how they do the task; they rarely say it unprompted, because to them it's obvious. Your job is to make the obvious explicit.

## Before the first question

Look at everything you can already see: the project's files, past work in the folder, their existing skills, what they said in this conversation. Your first question should already show what you found, so they correct your guess rather than start from nothing.

## How to ask

- **A single question, then wait.** A numbered list of questions reads like a form and gets short, careless answers. Ask, wait, and let the answer shape the next question.
- **Choices only for choices.** When the answer is one of a few (yes or no, this tone or that one, here or there), offer two to four concrete options drawn from what you saw, plus "something else". Use the tool's own way of asking if it has one; otherwise list them in plain chat.
- **Open for judgment.** "Take me through the last one, step by step", "is there one you were proud of?", "what would make you throw a draft away": these are theirs to answer in their own words. Never turn them into options. Your guesses would pull their answer toward yours, and the thing you're looking for is exactly what you couldn't guess.
- **Follow the surprise.** When an answer is unexpected, ask one more question about it. That's usually where the judgment is.

## What you need before you stop

| You need | Ask |
|---|---|
| The steps, in order | "Take me through the last one, step by step." |
| What they never do | "Which mistake would make you redo it from scratch?" |
| How it should sound | "Is there one you were proud of? Can I see it?" Then: "What makes this one right?" |
| What's always in, and always cut | "Is there anything every one of these has? Anything you always take out?" |
| The finish line | "What do you check before you call one finished?" |
| A real input | "What's the next one you'll actually need to do?" |

Chase every vague word. "Professional" or "clean" means nothing until there's an example or a contrast: "more like this one, or this one?"

## When to stop

Stop when you could hand them a result they would accept without edits. Short, rule-bound tasks take a handful of questions; anything that depends on how they sound takes many more. If after a real effort there's no judgment beyond what the model already does, that's the honest answer: say so in step 3.

## Using a past example well

When they share a past piece, read it for what they did without saying: length, structure, what comes first, the words they use and avoid, what's missing that you'd expect. Tell them what you noticed and ask whether each is a rule or a one-off. Only the rules go in the skill.
